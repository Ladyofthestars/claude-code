# You've Never Actually Touched Anything

YouTube video for Unbreakable Path, 16:9 (1920×1080, 60 fps), about 9 minutes, one calm narrator. Video 3 of the "made of stars" series: video 1 is where your atoms came from, video 2 is where they go, and this one is what they are like up close. Every fact is in `facts.md` with its source.

Tone: calm wonder, a little spiritual (we are never fully separate, never fully merged; we meet through forces), with a stoic close from Epictetus: our picture of the world is within our power. Every factual statement stays true and sourced. Institutions aren't quoted in the narration.

Structure: an opening hook (about 30 seconds) that starts with the most surprising true idea, 7 chapters that each answer one question and end on the next question, and an ending that returns to the opening line. Each chapter is one part of the animation (9 parts).

Voice: the channel voice (Kokoro, open source): am_michael 70% + am_onyx 30%, speed 1.02.

Look: the same glowing white drawings on deep blue as video 2, with gold highlights, drifting stardust and atoms as glowing dots, so the series looks like one.

## 1. Narration (word for word)

### Part 0: Opening
> You have never actually touched anything. Not the floor under your feet. Not this screen. Not even the hand of someone you love. The atoms of your body have never met the atoms of anything else.

> And yet, you feel everything. So what's really going on when you touch something? In this video, we'll go inside the atom, find out why you don't fall through the floor, and discover what touch really is.

### Part 1: What are you made of, really?
> Everything you can see and touch is made of atoms. Your skin, the air, the chair you're sitting on. An atom is about one ten-billionth of a meter across. That's so small it's hard to picture at all.

> Each atom has a nucleus at its center, made of protons and neutrons. Around it are electrons, tiny particles with a negative charge.

> So picture this instead. Imagine one atom blown up to the size of a football stadium. At the very center is the nucleus. And it would be about the size of a blueberry.

> The rest of the stadium is where the electrons are. They're so light that it would take about 1,800 of them to weigh as much as one proton. Yet they take up almost all of the atom's space.

> Almost all of an atom's weight is packed into that tiny blueberry. And almost all of its size is the space around it.

> So how do we know that atoms are mostly empty?

### Part 2: How do we know?
> A little over a hundred years ago, a physicist from New Zealand named Ernest Rutherford set up an experiment with two colleagues, Hans Geiger and Ernest Marsden.

> They fired tiny particles, called alpha particles, at a very thin sheet of gold foil. A glowing screen around it showed where each particle landed.

> Most of the particles went straight through, as if the gold wasn't there at all. But a very small number bounced almost straight back.

> Rutherford realized what it meant. The gold atoms had to be mostly empty space. Almost all of their mass was squeezed into a tiny, heavy center with a positive charge. Only the particles that came very close to it were knocked off course. It was a huge surprise. The solid world was mostly empty.

> So if atoms are mostly empty, why don't we fall right through the floor?

### Part 3: Why are things solid?
> The answer is in the electrons. Every atom is wrapped in them, and every electron has a negative charge. Atoms aren't hard little balls. They're more like tiny clouds of electric charge, and those clouds keep their distance.

> Charges of the same kind push each other away. So when the atoms of your feet come close to the atoms of the floor, their electrons push back against each other. The closer they get, the harder they push.

> There's a second rule too. Electrons can't all crowd into the same state. Scientists call it the exclusion principle, and it's part of what gives every atom its structure.

> Together, these rules make matter feel solid. You don't fall through the floor because, at the tiniest scale, the floor is pushing you away.

> So what really happens when you touch something?

### Part 4: What happens when you touch?
> Press your finger against a table. It feels like contact. But the atoms of your finger and the atoms of the table never merge. Their electrons push each other apart first.

> What you feel is that push. It's the electromagnetic force, the same force behind magnets and static electricity. Two magnets can push each other apart without ever touching. Electrons do the same.

> Comb your hair on a dry day, and the comb can pick up tiny scraps of paper without touching them. That's the same force, reaching across a gap.

> In fact, almost every force you feel in daily life is this one force. The grip of your hand, the friction under your shoes, the pull of a rope. All of it comes from atoms and molecules pushing and pulling on each other. The only exception is gravity.

> Rub your hands together and they get warm. That's the atoms of your skin clinging and letting go, setting each other vibrating, and the vibration turns into heat.

> So when you lean on a wall, what really holds you up is that push. The wall pushes back, atom by atom.

> But a push alone isn't a feeling. So how do you feel it?

### Part 5: How do you feel it?
> Your skin is full of tiny sensors. Some respond to pressure. Others respond to vibration, or to stretch. Some even notice when a single hair moves. Scientists call them mechanoreceptors.

> When the electrons of your fingertip push against the electrons of the table, your skin is pressed in, just a little. Those sensors notice, and they send a signal.

> The signal races up your nerves, through your spinal cord, to a relay station deep in your brain called the thalamus. From there, it goes to a strip of your brain called the somatosensory cortex. Touch from your face takes its own route, through nerves in your head.

> Only there does it become something you're aware of. The hard edge. The smooth surface. The warmth of another hand. Every touch you have ever felt was built inside your brain.

> So the world you touch is really a pattern of forces, translated by your brain. And if atoms are this empty, how much of you is actually there?

### Part 6: How much of you is empty?
> Almost all of your mass is packed into the nuclei of your atoms. Everything else is the space where your electrons are.

> There is a place in the universe where that space is gone. When a giant star dies, a core about the size of the Earth can collapse to less than 20 kilometers across, in less than a second. What's left can be a neutron star. Neutron stars are the densest objects in the universe. In effect, each one is a single, giant atomic nucleus. Its inside is about 95 percent neutrons, packed together like the inside of a nucleus.

> It's so dense that a piece the size of a sugar cube would weigh about a billion tons.

> Now think about every person alive today. There are about 8 billion of us. If you squeezed all of us as tightly as a neutron star, the whole of humanity would fit in less than a sugar cube.

> All that life, all that love, and almost all of it is space. So what does it really mean to touch someone?

### Part 7: What does it mean to touch someone?
> When you hold someone's hand, your atoms and theirs never merge. Their electrons and yours push back against each other.

> And yet, you feel them. Their warmth. Their grip. Your brain turns that push into one of the most powerful feelings a person can have.

> Maybe that's the deeper lesson. We are never fully separate, and never fully merged. We meet through forces. We feel each other across a space we cannot see.

> Almost two thousand years ago, the Stoic teacher Epictetus, who was born the son of a slave, wrote that men are disturbed not by things, but by the views which they take of things. His teaching begins with a simple idea: some things are within our power, and some things are not. In a way, science agrees with him. We never meet the world directly. We meet our own picture of it. And that picture is within our power.

> So if you've never touched anything, what has been touching you?

### Part 8: Ending
> Let's go back to where we started. You have never actually touched anything. The atoms of your body have never met the atoms of anything else.

> But you have felt everything. The ground has held you up every day of your life. Every hug, every handshake, every hand on your shoulder reached you. Not through contact, but through the same force that holds every atom together.

> And what you made of it, the comfort, the love, the strength, was built by you.

> So the next time you hold someone's hand, or feel the ground beneath your feet, remember what is really happening. Two worlds of atoms, almost entirely empty, holding each other up.

> You've never touched anything. And yet, you have been touched by everything.

## 4. Style line (word for word in every picture prompt)

Black and white scientific illustration, fine ink lines and soft graphite shading, like a page from an old textbook; one single object, isolated and centered.
