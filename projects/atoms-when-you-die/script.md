# What Happens to Your Atoms When You Die?

YouTube video for Unbreakable Path, 16:9 (1920×1080, 60 fps), about 10 minutes, one calm narrator. It is the follow-up to "Almost Every Atom in You Is Older Than the Earth": video 1 is where your atoms came from, and this one is where they go. Every fact is in `facts.md` with its source.

Tone: calm, reflective and a little spiritual (nothing is lost; you are a pattern, not a pile), with a stoic close from Marcus Aurelius. Every factual statement stays true and sourced. Institutions aren't quoted in the narration.

Structure: an opening hook (about 30 seconds) that starts with the most surprising true fact, 7 chapters that each answer one question and end on the next question, and an ending that returns to the opening and to video 1 ("You came from the stars"). Each chapter is one part of the animation (9 parts).

Voice: Kokoro (open source, Apache 2.0), voice A: am_michael 70% + am_onyx 30%, speed 1.02.

Look: the same hand-drawn science-book style as video 1, now on deep blue, with glowing white lines and text, warm gold highlights and drifting points of light. Atoms are shown as small glowing dots that flow between things, so the viewer can follow them.

## 1. Narration (word for word)

### Part 0: Opening
> Every breath you take holds, on average, about one molecule of air from Julius Caesar's last breath. The atoms in your hand right now may once have been part of a tree, a fish, or another person. Nothing in this world really disappears. And when you die, almost none of your atoms will be destroyed.

> So where will they go? In this video, we'll follow them. Into the soil, the air and the sea. Into other living things. Deep into the Earth. And finally, back to the stars.

### Part 1: Are your atoms really yours?
> Let's start with you, right now. You might think of your body as a thing that stays the same, like a statue. But it's more like a river. The water in a river is never the same from one moment to the next, and yet the river is still there.

> The red cells in your blood live for up to 120 days. Then they're broken down, and new ones take their place. Even your bones are rebuilt. Every year, about five to ten percent of your skeleton is taken apart and replaced with fresh bone.

> And every time you breathe out, you let go of carbon atoms that were part of your food. Your body burns that food for energy, and the carbon leaves you as carbon dioxide.

> So the atoms in you are always passing through. What stays is the pattern. The shape of you. The way you think. The way you love.

> So what happens to your atoms when the pattern stops?

### Part 2: What happens when you die?
> First, there's a rule that nature never breaks. When matter changes from one form into another, the total amount stays the same. Scientists call it the law of conservation of matter. Nothing is lost.

> After death, the work is taken over by some of the smallest living things on Earth: bacteria and fungi. They break down what is left, and they recycle it back into the living world. Every ecosystem on Earth depends on them. Without them, life would run out of the very materials it's built from.

> Your carbon is turned back into carbon dioxide, and much of it rises into the air. Some of it stays in the soil. The nitrogen in your body is turned into ammonium, and passed along through the ground. Your water returns to the sky and the sea.

> Nothing is destroyed. It's all simply handed on. So where does it go next?

### Part 3: Where does it go next?
> Let's follow one carbon atom. It drifts up into the air, as part of a molecule of carbon dioxide.

> One day, it reaches a leaf. Plants pull carbon dioxide out of the air, and with the energy of sunlight, they build it into sugar. Now your carbon atom is part of a tree, or a field of wheat. Almost every living thing depends on that sugar, one way or another.

> An animal eats the plant. A person eats the bread. And the atom that was once in you becomes part of someone else. Part of their blood, their muscles, maybe even their brain.

> Other carbon atoms sink into the ocean instead. The ocean is one of the biggest stores of carbon on Earth, and it swaps carbon back and forth with the air all the time.

> The air works the same way. Every breath you breathe out mixes into the air of the whole planet. That's why your breath holds a little of Caesar's. And one day, people who aren't born yet will breathe in a little of yours.

> But a few of your atoms do something different. They change. So which ones are they?

### Part 4: Which atoms change?
> Most of the carbon in your body is carbon-12. It's stable, and it can stay the same forever.

> But a very small part of it is carbon-14, a radioactive kind of carbon. It's made high in the sky, when rays from space strike nitrogen in the air. So even this part of you was made in the sky. While you're alive, you keep taking it in with your food, so the amount in you stays the same.

> When you die, that stops. And the carbon-14 slowly turns back into nitrogen. After 5,730 years, half of it is gone. After another 5,730 years, half of what's left.

> It's like a clock that starts the moment a life ends. Scientists read this clock to tell how long ago something lived. They call it radiocarbon dating, and it works for remains up to about 50,000 years old. It's how we know the age of ancient bones, wood and cloth.

> A few radioactive atoms like these slowly change. Almost everything else just moves on. And some of it moves very, very slowly. So where do your bones go?

### Part 5: Where do your bones go?
> Your bones are built mostly from calcium and phosphorus. Phosphorus is in your DNA too. And phosphorus is one of the slowest travelers on Earth.

> Rain and rivers carry it to the sea. There, it's taken up by sea life. When those creatures die, their remains sink, and settle on the ocean floor.

> Once it reaches the ocean, a phosphorus atom stays there for, on average, twenty thousand to a hundred thousand years.

> Calcium has a long road too. In the sea, it's built into shells. Over time, shells on the ocean floor become limestone. Limestone is the largest store of carbon on our planet.

> And over millions of years, the ground itself rises. Old sea floor is lifted up into land. Atoms that were once part of a body can end up in the stone of a mountain.

> Some carbon takes an even stranger road. When living things are buried deep underground, their carbon can be locked away for millions of years. That's how coal formed. The energy in a lump of coal today is sunlight that plants caught between 200 and 350 million years ago.

> So does the Earth keep our atoms forever?

### Part 6: Does the Earth keep them forever?
> For a very long time, yes. The Earth recycles itself. Sea floor carrying carbon is slowly dragged deep inside the planet, where one great plate of rock slides beneath another. And volcanoes breathe that carbon back out into the air.

> But the Earth won't last forever. The Sun is slowly getting brighter. In about one billion years, sunlight high in the sky will break water apart. And its hydrogen, made in the first few minutes of the universe, will escape into space. Our planet will start to look like Venus does today: dry, and much too hot for life as we know it.

> Billions of years from now, the Sun will swell into a red giant. It will swallow Mercury and Venus. Whether the Earth escapes is not certain. Many scientists think it will be swallowed too.

> It can feel strange to think about. But even then, nothing is destroyed. Even when the Earth is gone, its atoms will still be there.

> So what happens to all those atoms then?

### Part 7: Where do they go in the end?
> If the Earth is swallowed, its atoms would mix into the Sun's outer layers. And those are the very layers the Sun will let go of.

> A star like the Sun loses almost half of its mass as it dies. Its outer layers drift out into space as a glowing cloud. In the center, all that's left is a small, hot core called a white dwarf. There are tens of thousands of these glowing clouds in our galaxy right now.

> The gas from that cloud joins the thin clouds between the stars. It becomes raw material for everything new. One day, some of it will gather and collapse into a new star, with new planets around it. Just like our own solar system formed from the leftovers of older stars.

> And here's the beautiful part. The atoms a star gives back aren't the same as the ones it took in. Over its life, it has made new elements. So every time matter passes through a star, the universe becomes a little richer.

> And on one of those planets, maybe, some of those atoms will become part of something alive again.

> So, what does all of this mean for you?

### Part 8: Ending
> Let's go back to where we started. Every breath you take holds a little of the past. And when you die, almost none of your atoms will be destroyed.

> Some will rise into the air. Some will become trees, and animals, and other people. Some will rest for thousands of years at the bottom of the sea, or in the stone of a mountain. And one day, they may shine again inside a new star.

> Almost two thousand years ago, the Roman emperor Marcus Aurelius wrote that whatever dies cannot fall out of the world. Its elements are the world's elements. And when they are changed, they do not complain. So why should you?

> You came from the stars. And one day, you'll go back.
