# You Are Made of Stars

YouTube video, 16:9 (1920×1080, 60 fps), about 10 minutes, one calm narrator.
Topic chosen because no topic was given: where every atom in your body came from. Every fact is in `facts.md` with its source.

Structure: an opening hook (about 30 seconds), 7 chapters that each answer one question and end on the next question, and an ending that comes back to the opening line. Each chapter is one part of the animation (9 parts in all).

---

## 1. Narration (word for word)

Each `>` line is one paragraph. The voice is recorded one paragraph at a time and joined with short natural pauses (longer between parts).

### Part 0: Opening
> Almost every atom in your body is older than the Earth. The hydrogen in you was made in the first few minutes of the universe. The iron in your blood came from stars that blew up long ago and far away.

> So where did the rest of you come from? By the end of this video, you'll know where each part of you was made. And you'll see why it's fair to say that you are made of stars.

### Part 1: What are you made of?
> Let's start with you. Your body is built from tiny pieces called atoms. There are many kinds of atoms, called elements. But only a few of them do most of the work.

> By weight, the biggest part of you is oxygen. Next comes carbon. Then hydrogen. Then nitrogen. Add phosphorus and sulfur, and these six elements make up more than 97 percent of your body.

> Each one has a job. Hydrogen and oxygen join to make the water inside you. Carbon is the backbone of the big molecules that build your cells. Nitrogen and phosphorus are part of your DNA. Calcium makes your bones hard. And a little iron in your blood carries oxygen around your body.

> None of these atoms were made inside you. You took them in with your food, your water and your air. And almost none of them were made on Earth either. So where were they made?

### Part 2: Where did the first atoms come from?
> To find out, we have to go back to the very beginning. About 13.8 billion years ago, the universe began with the Big Bang. At first, it was far too hot for atoms to exist. But as it spread out, it cooled.

> In the first few minutes, the universe made just three elements: hydrogen, helium, and a tiny bit of lithium. That was it. No carbon. No oxygen. No iron. Every heavier element had to wait for the stars.

> Now here's the strange part. Think about a glass of water. Every water molecule has two hydrogen atoms. And NASA says the hydrogen in your body came from the Big Bang. There's no other real source of it in the universe. So the hydrogen in you is about as old as the universe itself.

> But you can't build a person out of hydrogen and helium. You can't make bones or blood from them. So who made the rest? The answer is up in the sky every day.

### Part 3: What do stars do?
> Look at the Sun. It's a huge ball of hot gas, made mostly of hydrogen. Its own weight squeezes its center, harder and harder, until it gets hot enough for something special to happen.

> Hydrogen atoms crash into each other and join together. They become helium. This is called fusion. And each time it happens, a tiny bit of mass turns into energy.

> The numbers are huge. Every second, the Sun turns about 600 million tons of hydrogen into helium. About 4 million tons of that becomes energy. Just one kilogram of hydrogen, fused like this, would give enough electricity to power an American home for about 17,000 years. And all that energy is the sunlight that reaches your face.

> The Sun has been doing this for about 4.5 billion years. In a way, every star is a furnace. It takes light atoms and presses them into heavier ones. But a star like the Sun mostly makes helium. And you can't build bones out of helium. So where does the carbon come from?

### Part 4: Where does carbon come from?
> Carbon comes from stars that are growing old. When a star like the Sun runs low on hydrogen in its center, the core shrinks and heats up. Its outer layers spread out, and the star becomes a red giant.

> Deep inside, the core keeps getting hotter. At about 100 million degrees, something new can happen. Three helium atoms slam together and become one carbon atom.

> Why three, and not two? When two helium atoms join, what they make falls apart almost at once. It takes three at the same time to make something that lasts. Then a carbon atom can grab one more helium. That makes oxygen.

> In the end, the old star gently pushes its outer layers out into space. Some of the carbon it made drifts away into the gas between the stars. NASA puts it simply: the carbon in your body was made by fusion inside stars.

> But a star like the Sun can't get hot enough to go any further. So where does the iron in your blood come from?

### Part 5: Where does iron come from?
> For iron, you need a much bigger star. Stars many times heavier than the Sun burn hotter and faster. Their centers get hot enough to keep going.

> Carbon fuses into oxygen, neon and magnesium. Then into silicon, sulfur and calcium. And finally, into iron. The star ends up built in layers, with the heaviest elements in the middle. Near the end, some of these steps take only months, or even days.

> But iron is a dead end. Up to iron, every step of fusion gives off energy. Iron doesn't. So when the core fills up with iron, the fire goes out, and nothing holds the star up anymore.

> What happens next is fast. In less than a second, a core about the size of the Earth collapses to less than 20 kilometers across. Then the rest of the star is blown apart. This is a supernova.

> For a short time, one exploding star can outshine its whole galaxy. The brightest ones shine about 10 billion times brighter than the Sun. Their gas flies out at around 10,000 kilometers every second. The blast throws carbon, oxygen, calcium and iron out into space. NASA says much of the iron in your blood was made in explosions like this, long ago and far away.

> But the story doesn't end there. The crushed core left behind is one of the strangest things in the universe. And it may be where gold comes from.

### Part 6: Where does gold come from?
> That crushed core can become a neutron star. It holds about as much mass as the Sun, but it's only about the size of a city. It's packed so tightly that a piece the size of a sugar cube would weigh about a billion tons.

> Sometimes, two neutron stars circle each other. Little by little, they spiral closer. And then, they crash.

> On August 17th, 2017, scientists caught this happening for the first time. Detectors in the United States and Italy picked up ripples in space itself, called gravitational waves. About two seconds later, space telescopes caught a short burst of gamma rays from the same part of the sky. Then telescopes around the world saw the light from the crash, about 130 million light-years away.

> In that light, they found signs of heavy elements being made, including gold and platinum. NASA says the gold in your jewelry was likely made in crashes like this. Scientists are still working out how much comes from these crashes, and how much from other kinds of exploding stars.

> So now we have hydrogen, carbon, oxygen, iron and gold, all scattered across space. How did they end up here, in you?

### Part 7: How did it end up in you?
> The space between the stars isn't empty. It holds thin clouds of gas and dust. Every time a star dies, it adds new atoms to these clouds.

> About 4.5 billion years ago, one of these clouds began to collapse. Most of it fell into the center and became our Sun. The rest spun into a flat disk around it. Inside that disk, tiny grains of dust stuck together, then grew into rocks, and then into planets. One of them was Earth. We can even check the date. The oldest meteorites, leftover building blocks from that disk, are about 4.5 billion years old.

> So the Earth, and everything on it, was built from the leftovers of older stars. Since then, the same atoms have been passed around again and again. A carbon atom might spend time in a rock, then in the air, then in a plant, then in an apple, and then in you.

> And the stars are made of the same ingredients as us. In 2017, astronomers measured the elements in more than 150,000 stars across our galaxy. They found the same six main elements of life that make up more than 97 percent of your body.

> So, what does it really mean to be made of stars?

### Part 8: Ending
> Let's go back to where we started. Almost every atom in your body is older than the Earth. The hydrogen in your water came from the first few minutes of the universe. The carbon in your cells was made inside old stars.

> The calcium in your bones and the iron in your blood were thrown out by exploding stars. And the gold in a ring may have come from two dead stars crashing together.

> Carl Sagan said it best. We are made of star-stuff. So the next time you look up at the night sky, remember: you're not just looking at the stars. You're looking at where you came from.
